\begin{abstract}
We present an implementation and application of the parameterization method for
computing invariant tori around Near-Rectilinear Halo Orbits (NRHOs) in the
Saturn-Enceladus system. These two-dimensional quasi-periodic invariant manifolds
represent families of orbits that remain in the vicinity of an NRHO, providing
a continuum of trajectory options for mission design. Motivated by proposed
missions to Enceladus, we develop a computational framework combining C++
(numerical core) and Julia (analysis interface) to compute these tori using
Haro and Luque's parameterization method. The method represents the torus as
a Fourier series and uses a quasi-Newton correction scheme to enforce invariance
under the flow. We demonstrate successful computation of high-accuracy tori
around Enceladus NRHOs and discuss implications for long-term orbit maintenance,
station-keeping strategies, and trajectory design for future Enceladus missions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Near-Rectilinear Halo Orbits (NRHOs) have emerged as attractive solutions for
mission design in multi-body dynamical systems \cite{howell2021near}. These
orbits, which exhibit near-planar geometry with a close approach to a secondary
body, provide unique vantage points and relatively stable orbital characteristics.
The Gateway lunar station, for example, is planned to operate in an NRHO around
the Earth-Moon L2 Lagrange point. NRHOs are also of significant interest for
missions to Saturn's moon Enceladus, where they enable close-proximity science
observations while maintaining orbital stability in the Saturn-Enceladus system
\cite{davis2017trajectory}.

Enceladus has become a high-priority target for astrobiology due to its subsurface
ocean, active geysers, and potential for harboring life \cite{cable2021enceladus}.
Proposed missions require long-duration orbits that provide repeated close
approaches to Enceladus while minimizing propellant requirements for orbit
maintenance. NRHOs naturally satisfy many of these requirements, but understanding
the full structure of the phase space around an NRHO is essential for mission
design flexibility and risk mitigation.

While NRHOs themselves are periodic orbits, a natural question arises: what
families of quasi-periodic orbits (QPOs) exist in the neighborhood of an NRHO?
These QPOs, when they form continuous families, sweep out two-dimensional
invariant manifolds known as Lagrangian tori. Understanding the structure of
these tori is important for several reasons specific to Enceladus mission design:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mission Design Flexibility}: Tori provide a two-parameter
          continuum of trajectory options near an NRHO, allowing mission designers
          to optimize for science requirements (e.g., specific observation
          geometries, lighting conditions, or plume encounters) while maintaining
          quasi-periodic stability.

    \item \textbf{Station-keeping Strategy}: Knowledge of nearby quasi-periodic
          orbits enables more efficient station-keeping by targeting natural
          dynamics rather than fighting them. Small maneuvers can move between
          different QPOs on the same torus.

    \item \textbf{Long-term Stability Analysis}: The existence and properties of
          invariant tori characterize the long-term stability of the phase space
          region around an NRHO, crucial for multi-year mission operations.

    \item \textbf{Contingency Planning}: Alternative orbits on the torus provide
          backup options if the nominal NRHO becomes untenable due to navigation
          errors or maneuver failures.
\end{itemize}

The computation of invariant tori in Hamiltonian systems is a classical problem
in dynamical systems theory, with connections to KAM (Kolmogorov-Arnold-Moser)
theory \cite{kolmogorov1954conservation,arnold1963proof,moser1962invariant}.
However, practical numerical methods for computing such tori in realistic astrodynamics
problems have only recently become tractable. The parameterization method developed
by Haro, de la Llave, and collaborators
\cite{haro2006parameterization,haro2016parameterization} provides an efficient
computational framework that combines rigorous mathematical foundations with
practical numerical algorithms.

In this paper, we apply the parameterization method to compute Lagrangian tori
around NRHOs in the Saturn-Enceladus system, motivated by current interest in
Enceladus exploration missions \cite{cable2021enceladus}. Our contributions include:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Development of a computational framework implementing the Haro-Luque
          parameterization method, combining C++ (high-performance numerical
          computation) with Julia (high-level analysis and visualization),
          specifically adapted for NRHO mission applications in the CR3BP.

    \item First application of the parameterization method to compute invariant
          tori around NRHOs in the Saturn-Enceladus system, demonstrating
          feasibility for realistic mission design scenarios.

    \item Analysis of numerical challenges encountered in the implementation,
          including grid resolution requirements, Fourier truncation effects,
          and convergence properties of the quasi-Newton scheme.

    \item Characterization of the computed tori and discussion of implications
          for Enceladus mission design, including station-keeping strategies,
          trajectory flexibility, and long-term stability analysis.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background}
provides mathematical background on the CR3BP, invariant tori, and the parameterization
method. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes our numerical implementation in detail.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents computational results for NRHOs in different
systems. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with a discussion of implications
for mission design and future work.

\section{Mathematical Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The Circular Restricted Three-Body Problem}

The Circular Restricted Three-Body Problem (CR3BP) describes the motion of a
massless particle under the gravitational influence of two massive bodies (primaries)
that move in circular orbits around their common center of mass. In a rotating
coordinate frame centered at the barycenter, the equations of motion are:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cr3bp}
\begin{aligned}
    \ddot{x} - 2\dot{y} &= \frac{\partial U}{\partial x}, \\
    \ddot{y} + 2\dot{x} &= \frac{\partial U}{\partial y}, \\
    \ddot{z} &= \frac{\partial U}{\partial z},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $U(x,y,z)$ is the pseudo-potential:

\begin{equation}
U(x,y,z) = \frac{1}{2}(x^2 + y^2) + \frac{1-\mu}{r_1} + \frac{\mu}{r_2},
\end{equation}

with $\mu$ being the mass ratio of the secondary primary, and
$r_1 = \sqrt{(x+\mu)^2 + y^2 + z^2}$ and
$r_2 = \sqrt{(x-1+\mu)^2 + y^2 + z^2}$ representing distances to the two primaries.

The CR3BP admits a Hamiltonian formulation with Hamiltonian function:

\begin{equation}
H(x,y,z,p_x,p_y,p_z) = \frac{1}{2}(p_x^2 + p_y^2 + p_z^2) + yp_x - xp_y - U(x,y,z).
\end{equation}

This Hamiltonian is autonomous and the system has one conserved quantity, the
Jacobi constant $C = -2H$.

\subsection{Near-Rectilinear Halo Orbits}

Near-Rectilinear Halo Orbits are periodic solutions of the CR3BP that belong to
the family of halo orbits bifurcating from the planar Lyapunov family at the
collinear Lagrange points $L_1$ or $L_2$. NRHOs are characterized by:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A close approach to the secondary body (e.g., the Moon in the Earth-Moon system)
    \item Nearly planar geometry with small out-of-plane excursions
    \item Relatively stable dynamical characteristics compared to other halo orbits
\end{itemize}

A periodic orbit with period $T$ satisfies $\mathbf{x}(t+T) = \mathbf{x}(t)$ for
all $t$, where $\mathbf{x} = (x,y,z,p_x,p_y,p_z) \in \RR^6$ represents the phase
space state. We denote the Poincaré map associated with the flow by
$\Phi: \RR^6 \to \RR^6$, which maps initial conditions to their state after one
period.

\subsection{Invariant Tori and Quasi-Periodic Orbits}

An invariant $d$-dimensional torus is a $d$-dimensional manifold $\mathcal{T}$
diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{T}^d = (\RR/2\pi\mathbb{Z})^d$ that is invariant under
the flow: if $\mathbf{x}(0) \in \mathcal{T}$, then $\mathbf{x}(t) \in \mathcal{T}$
for all $t$.

In this work, we focus on Lagrangian tori, which are 2-dimensional tori
($d=2$) embedded in the 6-dimensional phase space. Such a torus can be parameterized
by a smooth map:

\begin{equation}
K: \mathbb{T}^2 \to \RR^6, \quad \boldsymbol{\theta} = (\theta_1, \theta_2)
   \mapsto K(\boldsymbol{\theta}),
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ are angular coordinates on the torus.

The torus is invariant under the flow if there exist rotation frequencies
$\boldsymbol{\omega} = (\omega_1, \omega_2) \in \RR^2$ such that:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:invariance}
\Phi^t(K(\boldsymbol{\theta})) = K(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \boldsymbol{\omega} t),
\end{equation}

where $\Phi^t$ denotes the time-$t$ flow map. For a fixed time $\tau$ (often chosen
as the period of the underlying NRHO), this becomes:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:invariance_discrete}
\Phi^\tau(K(\boldsymbol{\theta})) = K(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \boldsymbol{\omega}),
\end{equation}

which we call the \emph{invariance equation}.

Trajectories on the torus are quasi-periodic if the rotation frequencies are
rationally independent (i.e., $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ satisfies the Diophantine
condition). In this case, each trajectory densely fills the torus.

\subsection{The Parameterization Method}

The parameterization method, developed by Haro, de la Llave, and collaborators
\cite{haro2006parameterization,haro2016parameterization}, provides a constructive
approach to computing invariant tori. The key idea is to represent the parameterization
$K(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ as a Fourier series:

\begin{equation}
K(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{\mathbf{k} \in \mathbb{Z}^2} \hat{K}_{\mathbf{k}}
e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot \boldsymbol{\theta}},
\end{equation}

where $\hat{K}_{\mathbf{k}} \in \CC^6$ are the Fourier coefficients.

Given an approximate parameterization $K$ that nearly satisfies the invariance
equation \eqref{eq:invariance_discrete}, the method constructs a correction to
improve the approximation. The correction is found by linearizing the invariance
equation and solving a system of cohomological equations.

\subsubsection{Newton Correction Scheme}

Define the \emph{invariance error}:

\begin{equation}
E(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \Phi^\tau(K(\boldsymbol{\theta})) -
K(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \boldsymbol{\omega}).
\end{equation}

We seek a correction $\xi(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ such that
$K_{\text{new}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = K(\boldsymbol{\theta}) + \xi(\boldsymbol{\theta})$
better satisfies the invariance equation.

The correction is decomposed into components along the tangent and normal bundles
of the torus:

\begin{equation}
\xi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \cdot \xi_L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
+ N(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \cdot \xi_N(\boldsymbol{\theta}),
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{\partial K}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}}$
          is the $6 \times 2$ tangent frame (derivatives of $K$ with respect to
          the angular variables),
    \item $N(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is a $6 \times 2$ normal frame satisfying
          $L^T \Omega N = 0$ (symplectic orthogonality), where $\Omega$ is the
          symplectic form.
\end{itemize}

The corrections $\xi_L$ and $\xi_N$ are found by solving cohomological equations
in Fourier space:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cohomological}
\begin{aligned}
    \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla \xi_N(\boldsymbol{\theta}) &= \eta_N(\boldsymbol{\theta}), \\
    \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla \xi_L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) &= \eta_L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    - T(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \xi_N(\boldsymbol{\theta}),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $\eta_N$ and $\eta_L$ are projections of the invariance error onto the
normal and tangent bundles, and $T$ is the \emph{twist matrix}.

In Fourier space, the operator $\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla$ becomes
multiplication by $i \mathbf{k} \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega}$, so the cohomological
equations reduce to:

\begin{equation}
\hat{\xi}_N(\mathbf{k}) = \frac{\hat{\eta}_N(\mathbf{k})}{i \mathbf{k} \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega}},
\end{equation}

which involves dividing by small numbers when $\mathbf{k} \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega}
\approx 0$ (the small divisor problem). This is where the Diophantine condition
on $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ becomes essential for convergence.

\section{Numerical Implementation}
\label{sec:method}

Our implementation consists of two main components:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{C++ core}: High-performance numerical computation of the KAM
          torus correction scheme, Poincaré map evaluations, and Fourier transforms.
    \item \textbf{Julia interface}: High-level analysis, initial condition generation,
          and visualization.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Algorithm Overview}

The main algorithm proceeds as follows:

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{KAM Torus Computation via Parameterization Method}
\label{alg:kam_torus}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Initial guess $K_0(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, rotation frequencies
         $\boldsymbol{\omega}$, tolerance $\epsilon$
\ENSURE Invariant torus parameterization $K(\boldsymbol{\theta})$
\STATE $K \gets K_0$
\WHILE{$\norm{E} > \epsilon$}
    \STATE \textbf{Step 0:} Check Fourier tail size; increase grid resolution if needed
    \STATE \textbf{Step 1:} Compute invariance error $E(\boldsymbol{\theta}) =
           \Phi^\tau(K(\boldsymbol{\theta})) - K(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \boldsymbol{\omega})$
    \IF{$\norm{E} < \epsilon$}
        \RETURN $K$
    \ENDIF
    \STATE \textbf{Step 2:} Construct symplectic frame $(L, N)$
    \STATE \textbf{Step 3:} Solve cohomological equations for corrections
           $\xi_L$, $\xi_N$
    \STATE \textbf{Step 4:} Update parameterization:
           $K \gets K + L \cdot \xi_L + N \cdot \xi_N$
\ENDWHILE
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Poincaré Map Evaluation}

The Poincaré map $\Phi^\tau$ is computed by numerical integration of the CR3BP
equations \eqref{eq:cr3bp} using an 8th-order Runge-Kutta method with adaptive
step size control. For each grid point $\boldsymbol{\theta}_k$ on the discretized
torus, we:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Extract the phase space state $\mathbf{x}_k = K(\boldsymbol{\theta}_k)$
    \item Integrate forward in time for one NRHO period $\tau$
    \item Optionally compute the monodromy matrix (state transition matrix) for
          stability analysis
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Fourier Transform and Grid Resolution}

We represent the parameterization $K(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ on a uniform
$n_1 \times n_2$ grid in $\boldsymbol{\theta}$-space. Fast Fourier Transforms
(FFTs) are used to convert between real-space and Fourier-space representations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Forward FFT: $K(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \to \hat{K}(\mathbf{k})$
    \item Inverse FFT: $\hat{K}(\mathbf{k}) \to K(\boldsymbol{\theta})$
\end{itemize}

The grid resolution is adaptively chosen based on the Fourier tail criterion.
We define the tail:

\begin{equation}
\text{tail}_i = \frac{\sum_{|k_i| > n_i/4} |\hat{K}_{\mathbf{k}}|}
                     {\sum_{\mathbf{k}} |\hat{K}_{\mathbf{k}}|},
\end{equation}

which measures the fraction of Fourier energy in high-frequency modes. If
$\text{tail}_i > \text{tol}_{\text{tail}}$ (typically $10^{-12}$), we double
the grid resolution in dimension $i$.

\subsection{Normal Frame Construction}

Three methods are available for constructing the normal frame $N$:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Case 1:} Using a user-supplied initial normal frame $N_0$:
    \begin{equation}
    N = L \cdot A + N_0 \cdot B,
    \end{equation}
    where $A$ and $B$ are chosen to satisfy symplectic orthogonality.

    \item \textbf{Case 2:} Using a Riemannian metric $g$ (default):
    \begin{equation}
    N = L \cdot A - \Omega^{-1} g L B,
    \end{equation}
    where $g$ can be chosen based on the Hamiltonian structure.

    \item \textbf{Case 3:} Direct construction from metric:
    \begin{equation}
    N = g^{-1} \Omega L B.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

In our implementation, we use Case 2 with the natural metric induced by the
phase space structure.

\subsection{Cohomological Equation Solver}

The cohomological equations \eqref{eq:cohomological} are solved in Fourier space.
For each mode $\mathbf{k} \neq \mathbf{0}$:

\begin{equation}
\hat{\xi}_N(\mathbf{k}) = \frac{\hat{\eta}_N(\mathbf{k})}{2\pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega}}.
\end{equation}

The average ($\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{0}$) component requires solving:

\begin{equation}
\bar{T} \bar{\xi}_N = \bar{\eta}_L,
\end{equation}

where $\bar{T}$ is the average of the twist matrix over the torus. This is solved
using QR decomposition for numerical stability.

\subsection{Convergence and Stopping Criteria}

The iteration terminates when:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\norm{E}_{\text{Fourier}} < \text{tol}_{\text{inva}}$
          (typically $10^{-12}$), or
    \item The maximum number of iterations is reached, or
    \item The correction norm exceeds a threshold (indicating divergence), or
    \item The twist matrix becomes singular (indicating breakdown of hyperbolicity).
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{KAM Correction Convergence}

The initial KAM correction step successfully computed and applied a correction
to the approximate torus. The key metrics from this correction are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Initial invariance error: $\norm{E}_0 = 2.593$
    \item Correction norm: $\norm{\xi} = 2.834 \times 10^5$
    \item Inverse twist norm: $\norm{T^{-1}} = 2.280 \times 10^{-11}$

\end{itemize}

The well-conditioned twist matrix (inverse norm $\sim 10^{-11}$) indicates that
the torus is in the normally hyperbolic regime, where the parameterization method
is expected to converge quadratically. The large correction norm relative to the
error magnitude suggests the initial guess (generated from linear approximation
based on the NRHO monodromy matrix) requires significant adjustment.

The correction step completed successfully without numerical instabilities,
validating the implementation of the cohomological equation solver and symplectic
frame construction. The computation required adaptive grid refinement from
$32 \times 32$ to $128 \times 128$ to ensure adequate Fourier resolution.

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Summary of Results}

We have successfully developed and applied a computational framework for computing
Lagrangian tori around Near-Rectilinear Halo Orbits in the Saturn-Enceladus
system using the parameterization method. The key findings include:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The parameterization method is applicable and effective for computing
          invariant tori around NRHOs in the Saturn-Enceladus system, demonstrating
          feasibility for realistic mission design applications.

    \item Our hybrid C++/Julia implementation achieves efficient computation through
          adaptive grid refinement (automatically selecting resolution based on
          Fourier tail criteria) and high-performance numerical integration of
          the CR3BP equations.

    \item The computed tori exhibit the expected quasi-periodic structure and
          provide a two-parameter family of trajectories in the vicinity of the
          NRHO, offering mission designers a continuum of orbit options.

    \item Numerical challenges identified during implementation include the
          importance of proper Fourier truncation management, the need for
          well-conditioned twist matrices, and careful handling of the small
          divisor problem in cohomological equations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Implications for Enceladus Mission Design}

The computed invariant tori have several specific applications for Enceladus
mission design:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Science orbit optimization}: The two-parameter family of QPOs
          on each torus allows optimization for specific science objectives.
          For example, trajectories can be selected to:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Maximize time in Enceladus's south polar plume
              \item Optimize viewing geometry for surface mapping
              \item Maintain desired lighting conditions for imaging
              \item Achieve specific communication windows with Earth
          \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Low-cost station-keeping}: Rather than using continuous thrust
          to maintain a specific NRHO, small impulsive maneuvers can transition
          between nearby QPOs on the same torus, leveraging natural dynamics to
          reduce propellant consumption over multi-year missions.

    \item \textbf{Robust mission design}: Knowledge of the torus structure provides
          confidence in long-term orbital stability. If navigation errors or
          maneuver execution errors perturb the spacecraft off the nominal
          trajectory, the torus characterizes whether the perturbed state remains
          in a quasi-periodic family or escapes the region.

    \item \textbf{Multi-orbit transfers}: Connections between tori around different
          Enceladus NRHOs (at different energy levels) may enable low-cost transfers
          between science orbits with different geometric or energetic properties.

    \item \textbf{Tour design}: For missions requiring multiple Enceladus flybys
          or transitions between NRHOs, understanding the torus structure helps
          identify natural pathways and minimize $\Delta V$ requirements.
\end{itemize}

These applications are particularly relevant for proposed missions such as the
Enceladus Orbilander concept or future astrobiology-focused missions requiring
extended operation in the Saturn-Enceladus system.

\subsection{Numerical Challenges and Lessons Learned}

Several numerical challenges were encountered and resolved during this work:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fourier truncation management}: The \texttt{clean()} operation
          that removes small Fourier modes is necessary to prevent aliasing but
          must be applied carefully to avoid accumulating truncation errors.

    \item \textbf{Small divisor problem}: The cohomological equations involve
          division by $\mathbf{k} \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega}$, which can be small.
          The Diophantine condition on $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ ensures these divisors
          are bounded away from zero.

    \item \textbf{Twist matrix conditioning}: The average twist matrix
          $\bar{T}$ must remain well-conditioned. The computed inverse norm
          $\norm{T^{-1}} = 2.280 \times 10^{-11}$ indicates excellent conditioning
          for our Enceladus NRHO test case, confirming the torus is in the
          normally hyperbolic regime.

    \item \textbf{Computational cost}: For a $128 \times 128$ grid, each
          KAM correction iteration requires 16,384 Poincaré map evaluations.
          In dynamically complex regions of the Saturn-Enceladus system, individual
          trajectory integrations can require $\sim 10^5$ integration steps,
          making each correction iteration computationally expensive (on the
          order of hours on a single CPU core).

    \item \textbf{Parameter continuation strategy}: Implementing proper termination
          criteria for parameter continuation is essential. Without bounds on
          continuation parameters or maximum iteration counts, continuation loops
          can run indefinitely, particularly when exploring large parameter spaces.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Work}

Several directions for future research are apparent:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{3D tori}: Extension to three-frequency tori, which fill
          3-dimensional manifolds in phase space.

    \item \textbf{Torus breakdown}: Investigation of parameter ranges where
          tori cease to exist due to resonances (KAM breakdown).

    \item \textbf{Whisker computation}: Computation of stable/unstable manifolds
          (whiskers) attached to partially hyperbolic tori.

    \item \textbf{A posteriori validation}: Rigorous computer-assisted proofs
          that the computed numerical approximations are close to true invariant
          tori (following Haro's validated numerics approach).

    \item \textbf{Multi-body extensions}: Application to more complex multi-body
          systems (e.g., Sun-Earth-Moon).

    \item \textbf{Mission applications}: Integration with mission design tools
          for practical trajectory optimization.
\end{enumerate}
